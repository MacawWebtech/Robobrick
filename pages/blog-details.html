5 Ways Robotics Builds Problem-Solving Skills

Maya SharmaLead Robotics Instructor
On this page1. Bugs are honest feedback2. Big problems get broken down3. Testing beats guessing4. Constraints spark ideas5. Explaining is understandingTry it at home

Every Saturday, someone's robot drives straight into a wall. What happens in the next five minutes is where the real learning lives.

Parents often ask us whether robotics is about preparing kids for engineering careers. It can be. But after teaching more than 1,800 children, we think the bigger payoff is a habit of mind: when something doesn't work, you investigate instead of giving up. Here's how that habit gets built.

1. Bugs are honest feedback

A worksheet can be marked wrong without a child understanding why. A robot that turns right instead of left shows the mistake immediately and physically. There's no arguing with it, and no teacher's opinion involved. That makes failure feel less personal and more like information.

2. Big problems get broken down

“Build a rover that collects a sample” is overwhelming. “Make the wheels turn,” then “make it stop at the line,” then “lower the scoop” is not. Kids learn to decompose a goal into steps they can test one at a time, the same way software engineers work.

The question we ask most often isn't “what's the answer?” It's “what's the smallest thing you could test right now?”

3. Testing beats guessing

Early on, children change five things at once and hope. By week six, most have learned to change one thing, run the robot, and note what happened. That's the scientific method, discovered through frustration rather than memorised from a textbook.

4. Constraints spark ideas

Our challenges always include a limit: only 40 bricks, only one motor, must fit in a shoebox. Constraints push kids past the first obvious idea and toward clever ones. Some of our best builds came from the strictest rules.

5. Explaining is understanding

At Demo Day, every child explains their build to a room of adults. To do that, they have to understand their own choices. The kid who can say “I used a smaller gear because the arm kept dropping things” has learned more than the one who just made it work.

Try it at home

  1. Give your child 30 bricks and one challenge: build something that rolls down a ramp the furthest.
  2. Ask them to predict the distance before each test.
  3. Let them change only one thing between tests, and write it down.
  4. After five tests, ask: what made the biggest difference?
